# Literature Search

## Inputs and scope

Accept the research question, purpose, existing paper lists or other material,
supplements, applicable standards, date range, output requirements and location
in natural language or an external file. Read the supplied material, current
project instructions and applicable research and document-presentation standards
before searching. Use existing lists as seeds and a deduplication baseline.
Report which inputs were used.
Selected-paper analyses and existing asset indexes are optional inputs: reuse
what they establish, retaining their actual source versions and verification
limits. A report standard organizes delivery without exhausting the scientific
questions; retain task-relevant evidence and reusable assets even when its
example outline omits them.
Project-specific rules stay external, without requiring a company account or
private path. If a requested input is inaccessible, state the affected scope and
continue independent work; do not claim compliance with unread rules. State
reasonable assumptions for unspecified details rather than requiring a form.

Determine the requested deliverable from the task. A search/list request may end
with screened candidates. A literature review, survey or research-landscape
request requires a synthesis in prose; a paper table or search validation report
alone does not satisfy it. Use the applicable project/review standard, keeping
its field-specific structure and naming external. Preserve an existing outline
and review as the starting point when updating a topic.

Keep discovery, deep reading, library maintenance and reproduction distinct.
Searching does not itself authorize reorganizing existing papers, running
experiments, uploading documents or sending messages. Reuse existing outputs
where appropriate and follow the requested read/write boundaries.

## Search and selection

1. Decompose the question into concepts, synonyms, abbreviations and exclusions.
2. Build complementary query families and choose appropriate scholarly sources.
   Use seed references and citing work to fill important gaps, not an unbounded
   citation crawl. Record access failures and actual search coverage.
3. Screen by research relevance, citation impact, evidence quality, venue,
   recency and role in the field. Open the original paper or authoritative
   metadata page for included works; snippets and curated lists are discovery
   aids, not evidence of full-text reading.
4. Deduplicate by DOI/arXiv ID, title and authors. Link preprint and publication
   records, recording the version actually read; substantive extensions may be
   separate works. Do not merge different works by similar titles alone.
5. Separate surveys, foundational work, representative methods and recent
   advances. Explain inclusion and reading priority for the current purpose.
6. Stop when the scoped questions have useful coverage and further searches no
   longer change the main picture, or report the remaining gaps and limits.
   Do not claim exhaustive or systematic coverage without supporting records.

## From outline to a literature review

For a review/survey request, carry the work through the following stages at a
depth appropriate to its scope. Follow supplied standards over the fallback
organization below; do not copy a private standard into the distributed skill.

1. **Design the outline.** State questions, inclusion/exclusion boundaries,
   evidence cutoff, a stable mechanism-based taxonomy and comparison axes.
   Use surveys to map the field, then verify representative claims in primary
   sources. For a broad topic, save a substantive outline before drafting; a
   narrow question can use a compact outline within the review. Explain what
   each section answers, representative evidence, and useful diagrams/math.
2. **Read to support the synthesis.** Map core questions to foundational work,
   strong comparisons, turning points and recent direct neighbors. Read the
   relevant methods, experiments and supplements needed for each claim, beyond
   metadata/abstracts. Record actual versions, dates and locations. Seek negative
   results and competing explanations; a new related method can change the
   taxonomy or opportunity claim, so revise the outline when needed.
3. **Synthesize across papers.** Organize prose by common problem or method
   family. Develop the shared problem and mechanism, representative differences,
   evidence and costs, applicability limits and current judgment. Compare the
   data, models, algorithms, metrics, baselines, implementation and budget that
   matter to the claim. Separate author reports from independent checks.
   Do not turn each paper's abstract into a consecutive mini-summary.
4. **Explain the research trajectory.** Connect changes in the problem,
   assumptions or available capabilities to subsequent methods. A timeline is
   not a list of years; chronological proximity does not establish inheritance.
   Distinguish original writing-time questions from later progress as of the
   review cutoff. Check direct prior work before asserting a research gap.
5. **Write and reconcile the full text.** Unless a supplied structure differs,
   cover scope/definitions, research trajectory, method families, necessary
   cross-cutting axes, evaluation/comparison, synthesis/open questions and
   references. Keep the outline and body aligned. Write the executive summary
   after the body, reflecting its supported conclusions, disagreements and
   proposed next steps. Keep the summary focused on conclusions and the body on
   synthesis. Consolidate repeated search, verification and library-maintenance
   details in existing supporting records; retain brief scope limits where they
   affect interpretation. Preserve scientific settings and reusable-asset detail.
   Use a consistent abstraction and explain diagrams/math alongside the mechanism
   they describe.
6. **Check completion.** Confirm that the stated questions receive reasoned
   answers with source evidence and conditions, key comparisons use compatible
   protocols or explain incompatibility, and opportunity claims reflect the
   covered literature. Link representative sources to the existing topic index;
   update bibliography/library records only within the authorized scope.
   Distinguish a complete scoped review from exhaustive coverage or reproduction.
   Deliver the actual review Markdown and outline when required, with remaining
   coverage, reading and rendering limits. Verification logs support this
   deliverable rather than replacing it.

Citation counts and task-relative inclusion tiers stay visible in the review's
bibliography/index. They guide source selection without replacing the prose
synthesis. If missing access prevents a central comparison, name that gap and
limit the conclusion instead of presenting a complete-looking unsupported review.

## Connect paper analysis, synthesis and reusable research material

Preserve the original literature-search information: each selected paper's
objective, method, contribution, data, metrics, results and code, alongside
identity, publication/version, citation impact and task-relative inclusion.
The review standard organizes the report; it does not replace this scientific
content or the close-reading needed to establish it.

For core comparisons, use existing paper analyses as optional inputs. If a
needed claim or configuration is missing, perform targeted close analysis of
the relevant source, using an available paper-analysis skill when helpful or
equivalent evidence-based reading otherwise. Extract the problem/contribution,
method and applicable theory, experimental configuration and results, and
reusable Data, Model, Algorithm, Metrics, Baselines and Code/Implementation.
Retain source version, reading date, locations and verification depth.
Neither another installed skill nor a separate full note for every paper is
required. Reusing an analysis does not count as independently rereading it.

Carry this information through three connected uses:

1. **Explain the studies.** Link claims/results to actual models, data,
   algorithms, metrics, baselines and conditions. Distinguish what was used from
   what the work introduced, and actual experiments from cited results.
2. **Compare across studies.** Group by shared problem/mechanism and preserve
   configuration, split, protocol, tuning-budget and version differences that
   make results comparable or incomparable.
3. **Consolidate research resources.** In the report or an existing topic index,
   group repeated resources by concrete object and link them to the studies
   that used them. For useful items give their name/configuration, role, actual
   entry point (paper location or inspected artifact), intended reuse,
   conditions and missing prerequisites. Separate baselines already reported
   by a paper, results it cites, and controls proposed for this new study.

An asset list must support a research decision: what can be reused for which
experiment, and what still blocks reuse. Six category headings, generic advice
or a repository URL alone do not establish this. Preserve necessary details
such as task splits, model targets, evaluator aggregation or function/config
entry points when actually available. Distinguish paper-reported, artifact-
inspected, executed and reproduced evidence; do not invent downloadability,
licenses, revisions, commands or successful runs.

Use a proportionate form; do not impose six tables or a new database, duplicate
all paper analyses, force nonexistent assets onto theoretical/position work,
or run/download implementations without task authorization. Before delivery,
trace a representative core source through its analysis, cross-paper comparison
and a concrete reuse choice with explicit remaining conditions.

Follow the supplied citation style and keep its reader-facing labels mapped
to stable bibliography/index identities. Reformatting citations must preserve
the source and claim associations, versions and locations; internal IDs do not
by themselves define a publication or evidence category.

End a review with a readable bibliography: authors, complete official title,
year and venue/publication status, DOI or original link, and the version and
source actually read. Keep detailed reading locations and verification notes
in the existing reading table or evidence record rather than repeating them
in every bibliography entry. Use a consistent conventional reference format
without blanket title bolding or process commentary. For a requested compact
bibliography, keep entries consecutive with explicit line breaks instead of
blank paragraphs or separator rules. Preserve abbreviated author lists when only
those names are verified; do not invent missing coauthors or publication data.
A value-ranked selection table can accompany this bibliography. Bare URLs,
Markdown link definitions and that selection table alone do not replace it.
Check the final reference section as readers will see it, preserving citation
numbers and source targets when changing the Markdown representation.
Keep body markers compact according to the requested style. When number-only
citations are requested, use plain `[n]` and put source links in the bibliography.
Correcting the bibliography does not itself require changing body citation syntax.

## Citation impact is a prominent selection signal

Actively seek influential, highly cited work and make citation counts prominent
in the reading list. For foundational work and established methods, citation
impact should carry substantial weight in selection and reading priority.
Combine it with direct
relevance and what the paper contributes. Recent research has had less time to
accumulate citations, so explain that context rather than treating a low count
as automatic exclusion or a high count as proof that every claim is sound.

Retrieve counts from an identifiable bibliographic source when accessible.
Report the count, source link and observation date; a shared source/date note
can cover a whole table. Keep sources consistent where possible, or label
differences rather than treating cross-platform counts as directly comparable.
Confirm the count belongs to the correct paper/version group; do not sum
overlapping preprint/publication counts, substitute GitHub stars or infer a
number from a search snippet. If lookup fails, write “unavailable” and the
reason, not zero or an invented estimate, and continue the literature search.
Counts order exploration and reading; no automatic paper score is required.

Reconcile citation coverage with the included bibliography before delivery.
For requested fresh lookups, distinguish a successful observation, an attempted
lookup that failed, and work not attempted after an access restriction. If an
earlier count is retained, keep its real observation date and provenance rather
than dating it as refreshed. Reader-facing tables show counts, sources and dates;
batch labels such as "this run" or "reused" belong in supporting records.

## Inclusion value tiers

Give included papers an explicit task-relative tier and a short reason, rather
than only free-text priority. Use the supplied tier rubric if present; otherwise
use **A+**, **A**, **A-**, **B+**, **B**, **B-**, **C**:

- **A+ Decisive core:** a small set whose evidence materially determines the
  central novelty, method choice or acceptance criteria; read first.
- **A Main line:** central methods or indispensable foundations needed to
  understand and design the current study.
- **A- Necessary support:** closely supports the main line or its strongest
  alternatives, but does not itself determine the central research decision.
- **B+ Key comparison:** a specific, high-value comparison, evaluation protocol
  or adjacent route to examine for the corresponding design choice.
- **B Important reference:** useful evidence, assets or implementation conditions
  that can be consulted by research branch.
- **B- Branch reference:** indirect or optional material to revisit when its
  subquestion becomes active.
- **C Background:** broad context or peripheral detail with limited direct
  bearing on the present question.

Keep the tier separate from relationship to existing material (e.g. existing
seed or new candidate), the reason, actual reading depth and citation count with
source/date. Citation impact remains a substantial signal for mature work;
combine it with relevance and knowledge dependencies without converting counts
into fixed thresholds or a numerical score. Tiers describe value for this task,
not universal paper quality, venue rank or verification status. Abstract-only
judgments are provisional and can change after deeper reading. If the available
material cannot support a tier, mark it pending with the reason; do not force C.
Reserve the A bands for work the present study needs to understand closely;
being related, recent or foundational alone does not put every paper in A.
Use the B bands for substantial comparisons and references. If top bands become
crowded, revisit each paper's actual role rather than only adding plus signs.
Do not impose grade quotas or map citation-count quantiles to grades. Show the
tier and reason in the existing list, without a separate grading document.

Present a reading/selection table in task-relative value order, using the
supplied rubric or the ordered tiers above first. Within a tier, weigh relevance and
knowledge dependencies, citation impact, evidenced contribution differences
and recent developments. Give citation impact substantial weight for mature
foundations and comparable established methods; keep recent direct neighbors
that change the novelty or feasibility judgment prominent despite low counts.
A newer date alone does not establish novelty. Explain the main ordering
choices in a short note or the existing reasons column, without inventing a
weighted score. Keep citation labels and stable paper identities unchanged when
sorting rows. A chronological view serves a timeline rather than replacing
the default reading-priority order.

## Google Scholar with Browser Use

For a small citation lookup, use Google Scholar's visible website through an
available Browser Use tool when requested or when API counts are
unavailable. Follow that tool's current instructions; the skill does not bundle
a browser or promise that every host can access Scholar. Replace the search
field with the full title, verify the actual query, then match the result's
title, authors and original paper link before reading that row's “Cited by”.
Record its count, source/result link and observation date. Use the displayed
count, not the overall search-result estimate, and do not infer zero from a
missing citation link. Separate clusters or citation-only entries may overlap:
record the selected matching entry and note ambiguity rather than adding counts.
This is a limited interactive lookup, not a bulk scraping interface. Stop on a
CAPTCHA, access block or rate limit; leave affected counts pending or request
user handoff according to the tool's rules, without bypassing the restriction.

Query one paper at a time. Verify and save the result before submitting the
next title; for a sequence of lookups, wait at least 15 seconds between new
search submissions by default, or longer when the user or service requires it.
Do not batch rapid submissions or run parallel Scholar searches. This pacing
reduces request frequency but does not guarantee access or permit retries past
a CAPTCHA or access block.

## Full-text retrieval and library intake

When the task needs a PDF and the API returns only metadata, an abstract, or an
unusable download link, use Browser Use to open the DOI, publisher, venue, or
authoritative repository page and find an accessible PDF. Prefer the requested
version and record the version actually obtained. Use the browser's download
flow if the resulting file is accessible, or pass a verified direct PDF URL to
an available downloader. Stop at access controls and mark the PDF pending if
no permitted route yields a readable file.

Verify the downloaded file opens as a PDF and matches the paper and version.
When the task includes intake, reuse any matching copy and use the project's
confirmed library location, naming, and index rules. Follow the
[English](references/README.md#downloading-papers) or
[Chinese](references/README_zh.md#下载论文) guide for the handoff.

## Lightweight evidence and delivery

For a small task, keep the evidence inside the result: a short note with the
date, sources, key queries, time window and access/coverage limits, followed by
paper entries with title/authors, stable ID or original link, publication or
version date, relationship to existing material, inclusion value tier and reason,
reading status and citation count provenance.
Distinguish metadata/abstract screening from full-text assessment. Explain
important exclusions or uncertain matches when they affect the conclusion.
Do not require separate logs, a database, fixed paper counts or a file tree.
Larger or continuing reviews can reuse their existing search records.

Return a cited synthesis of the scoped question, prioritized reading list and
unresolved gaps. Keep source claims and your interpretation distinct. If the
task requests a full literature review, apply its supplied reporting standard
and state the actual synthesis and reading coverage.

Before delivery, recheck the applicable document conventions and read the
opening, dense passages, figure/table surroundings and final references in order.
Remove duplicated explanations and process commentary, and adjust headings,
paragraphs and emphasis to support a coherent argument while retaining necessary
evidence and conditions. Keep company-specific layout rules external. Report
content review, parsing and actual destination-preview checks separately with
their real scope; a successful parser or a local preview does not establish
compliance in an uninspected destination. Derivation checks remain distinct.
The [English guide](references/README.md) and
[Chinese guide](references/README_zh.md) are optional human tutorials, not
permission to execute all their follow-up suggestions.

## Diagrams and mathematical abstraction

Write all mathematical formulas in LaTeX, including inline and display equations.

When the requested output explains method families or mechanisms, keep a clear
text explanation and aim to accompany it with Mermaid diagrams and applicable
LaTeX abstractions. Show the same concept from complementary perspectives:
meaning and conditions in prose, structure and relationships in diagrams, and
objects, constraints, or quantitative relationships in mathematics. Explain how
these representations correspond and plan them together in any outline.
A search list alone does not need formulas or diagrams. Do not invent formulas
or imply deeper reading than was actually completed.

Explain nodes, arrows, symbols, dimensions, and assumptions. Cite the supporting
source locations and distinguish source content from your own abstraction.
Keep editable diagram source, check consistency with the text, and verify
rendering where supported; disclose unverified rendering. Follow the destination's
math and diagram conventions rather than forcing a diagram or formula quota.
